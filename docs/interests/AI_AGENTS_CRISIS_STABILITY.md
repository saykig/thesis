## Corrections and boundaries

| Starting assumption | More defensible formulation |
|---|---|
| Two nuclear players are stable; a third makes them unstable. | Bracken motivates examining multipolar interactions [1]. This is not a universal actor-count law. Crisis stability concerns incentives to initiate or escalate during a confrontation; mutual second-strike capability, beliefs and command arrangements matter. The Cold War “two-player” framing is an abstraction, not a literal inventory of nuclear states. |
| More AI agents mean more strategic players. | Count independent objectives and effective decision authority separately from software instances. A state running many agents may remain one strategic principal. Delegation can introduce additional operational discretion without creating an independent geopolitical actor. |
| Isolated states can simply use AI to conduct cyberattacks. | Replace “siloed” with a specified property: weak crisis communication, limited cooperation, attribution uncertainty or resistance to external pressure. These properties require evidence. Identify access, resources, permissions and human involvement; nationality or diplomatic isolation does not establish capability [4]. |
| Multiple actors have already been proven to cause instability. | The proposed causal relationship is unestablished here. Incident frequency, contagion/spillover, misattribution and interstate escalation are different outcomes. Demonstrating one does not establish the others. |
| Frequent threats make deterrence fade. | Frequency alone cannot identify deterrence failure. Define the actor, prohibited conduct and expected response. Frequent minor intrusions can coexist with restraint at a more consequential threshold. Nye explicitly cautions against expecting complete cyberattack prevention [2]. |
| No warning before an attack means there is no deterrence. | A warning before each operation is unnecessary if expected consequences are already understood. Separate an attacker's coercive warning from a defender's standing deterrent posture. An attack can be an action and a signal, and can be intended to influence subsequent conduct. Denial and resilience also matter [2]. |
| Propagation demonstrates that more actors cause crisis instability. | Malware spreading across systems is different from additional independent actors making decisions. Spillover, actor proliferation and strategic escalation need separate evidence. |

The crisis-stability definition and actor distinctions are analytical clarifications used in this note, not quotations from Bracken. Do not use “stability” interchangeably with peace, low incident frequency or successful containment.

## Candidate question and mechanism

**Assistant-proposed question:**

> Under what conditions does delegating cyber operations to AI agents weaken states' ability to identify responsibility and interrupt escalation involving an additional independent actor?

This retains Sara's interest without assuming that AI, actor proliferation and instability always move together. It extends Q26 from decision speed to attribution, delegation and third-party intervention.

**Proposed mechanism, not a finding:** delegated action plus uncertain responsibility may allow further operations before political authorities can clarify an incident, contact the relevant actor and halt a response. A third party could exploit or worsen the ambiguity. Conversely, better monitoring, defensive automation, bounded permissions and reliable communication could reduce that risk.

UNIDIR's November 2026 publication identifies compressed decision time and interactions between different operators' agents as concerns; its public summary says evidence is strongest in digital settings and more experimental in military command and physical systems [3]. This is a close research precedent, so “AI agents may complicate escalation control” is not itself a novelty claim.

Separate five explanatory variables before choosing any design:

- independent actor count;
- attribution confidence;
- scope of delegated authority and ability to recall an agent;
- time between detection, interpretation, authorization and response;
- incident frequency relative to monitoring and response capacity.

A **candidate conditional hypothesis** is that an additional independent actor increases misdirected responses primarily when attribution is weak and response authority permits action before clarification. A rival is that intelligence quality, defensive capacity or existing political hostility explains the outcome, with actor count adding little. Another rival is that AI improves detection and containment enough to offset faster offensive action.

**What would weaken the hypothesis?** Comparable episodes or controlled model conditions in which the extra actor does not increase mistaken responses; reliable attribution removes the effect; delegated systems preserve effective intervention; or defensive gains outweigh the proposed escalation pathway. These are proposed tests, not claims that such evidence has been collected.

## Evidence status and European scope

The February 2026 International AI Safety Report reviews emerging real-world AI-assisted cyber misuse [4]. It does not establish this note's actor-count mechanism. Its capability assessments are dated; they should not be presented as a November 2026 capability ceiling.

A European historical comparator is **NotPetya (2017)**. The UK government's February 2018 statement attributes the operation to the Russian military and describes effects beyond its primary Ukrainian targets [5]. This is an official attribution and account of spillover, not independent causal proof, an AI-agent case, or evidence that adding independent actors caused escalation. Affected organizations are not automatically additional strategic decision-makers.

For a 20-page CEES paper, the proposed scope is **one cyber crisis or a tightly bounded comparison with a European/Eurasian connection**. Russia–Ukraine-related infrastructure is a candidate search area, not a selected case. North Korea remains a comparative lead. The focus is cyber operations and crisis management.

**Next discriminating step:** audit candidate episodes for (a) independently supported AI involvement and its exact function, (b) an identifiable third actor, (c) a timeline of attribution and decisions, and (d) an observable escalation threshold. If that combination is unavailable, either study the older cyber mechanism without an AI causal claim or frame AI as a prospective, assumption-explicit extension.

A small simulation is optional and comes only after this audit. If warranted, compare two versus three independent actors while keeping aggregate attack resources and baseline preferences comparable; vary attribution and delegated response authority separately. Possible outputs are mistaken retaliation, defined escalation-threshold crossings and time to halt an action. Do not mechanically increase attack volume with actor count and then call the resulting incidents evidence of instability. Such a model would show consequences of its assumptions, not predict real warfare.
